# About Harold "Dugan" Elgar

For the first ten years of my life, Harold “Dugan” Elgar occupied an important place in my father’s side of the family. He was my grandfather Bill Nicely’s uncle, and everyone called him “Uncle Dugan.” My memories of him include that he had a koi pond he was proud of, loved Japanese art, and rode in every Fourth of July parade for the Shriners. I remember him as a very happy man who loved his wife dearly and thought the world of my grandparents.

## Early Life and Stone Carving Roots

Born in Bloomington, Indiana in 1910, Dugan attended a special vocational program through Bloomington High School to study stone carving as a teenager. My grandmother, Patricia Nicely, saved a photograph of some of the students she found in an old periodical. But, unfortunately, I have no way of knowing which one is Dugan (she passed away in 2009).

{% include feature/item-figure.html objectid="dugan_016" width="50%" position="left" %}

Bloomington (and Southern Indiana in general) had many active limestone quarries at the time, so stone carving would have been an appealing trade for many young boys. But because he had a passion and a talent for art, Dugan took those skills in an upward direction and decided to become a sculptor.

## World War II Service in the Seabees

First, however, his life—like so many others—got derailed by WWII. He joined the Navy in 1943 and served in the Seabees (25th Naval Construction Battalion) until 1946. Stationed in Guam, he worked as an engineer, and yet he still found time for artistic pursuits.

{% include feature/item-figure.html objectid="dugan_046" width="50%" position="right" caption="Illustration from the 25th Naval Construction Battalion cruise book, Guam." %}

## Post-War Sculpture and *Breaking Away*

Upon returning from Guam, Dugan eventually attended the John Herron Art Institute in Indianapolis to hone his skills as a stone sculptor. In 1968, for his senior project, he sculpted one of his most famous works: the *Pietà*. It remained in storage until his death in 1984, when it was finally installed at his gravesite.

{% include feature/item-figure.html objectid="dugan_035" width="50%" position="left" %}

Although he never achieved national fame as a sculptor, his works can be found in some remarkable places, including the National Cathedral in Washington, D.C., and the First Church of Christ, Scientist in Boston, Massachusetts. In his hometown of Bloomington, meanwhile, he was practically a star. In 1979, he played himself in the movie *Breaking Away*, which included the limestone industry in Bloomington as a backdrop. The term “Cutters” (the name of the all-townie bicycle team in the movie) derives from the fact that so many townspeople in Bloomington were tied to that industry.

{% include feature/item-figure.html objectid="dugan_005" width="50%" position="right" %}

## Community Legacy

With the exception of having a role in a famous Hollywood movie, however, the legacy Dugan leaves behind mirrors that of many of his peers. His works, overall, are not well known by the wider public. Hundreds of people probably pass by the *Red, Blonde, Black and Olive* monument at Miller-Showers Park between Walnut Street and College Avenue every single day without even knowing who carved it.

{% include feature/item-figure.html objectid="dugan_026" width="50%" position="left" %}

From start to finish, therefore, we can look at his life as an example of what it would have been like to live as a limestone sculptor and artist during the height of the limestone industry in Southern Indiana. He may have never achieved fame, but he left many works behind, which both researchers in Indiana history and burgeoning artists alike can study for years to come. He served his country during WWII, and he was an active member of the Bloomington Elks Lodge, the Masonic Lodge of Bloomington, the Scottish Rite of Indianapolis, the Murat Shrine of Indianapolis, the Stone Belt Shrine Club, and the Shrine Clown Unit.

But for me, his great-niece, he was also a loving family man who expended much energy on charitable causes and capped off his career by carving a monument (*Red, Blonde, Black and Olive*) that will forever symbolize Bloomington’s commitment to welcome and champion racial and cultural diversity in our city.
